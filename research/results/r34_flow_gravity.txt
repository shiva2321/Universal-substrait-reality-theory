    eps = 1.00: deflection = 0.041223 = 1.0000 of GR   (eps^2 = 1.0000)
    => log-log slope at small eps = 2.00: the bending goes as the SQUARE of the flow speed.
       First-order (linear in v) terms cancel for a symmetric inflow; the bending comes from v^2, the 'potential' part
       of the acoustic metric.  A flow produced linearly by the source, v ~ G M, would bend by ~ (G M)^2: far too little.
       Only a flow with v^2 ~ G M / r, i.e. v ~ sqrt(G M) (non-analytic in the source strength), matches gravity.

C. What would sustain the river flow in a conserved fluid?
   Continuity with uniform density rho and steady flow: div v = - s / rho, so the sink density needed is
       s(r) = rho (3/2) sqrt(2 G M) r^(-3/2)      (kg per second per cubic metre),
   and the total drain through a sphere of radius r is  Phi(r) = 4 pi rho sqrt(2 G M) r^(3/2).
   Phi GROWS with r: the sinks are spread through all space, not located in the body, and Phi is not proportional to M.
   Sun, medium at Planck density 5.15e+96 kg/m^3: drain through the Sun's surface 1.9e+121 kg/s, through 1 AU 6.1e+124 kg/s.
   Euler equation: for the river flow v . grad v = - G M / r^2, i.e. the fluid elements themselves accelerate inward at the
   Newtonian rate.  A steady irrotational flow of a uniform-density fluid has v . grad v = grad(v^2/2) balanced only by pressure
   gradients, which are zero for uniform density: the flow needs an external inward force equal to gravity to exist.
   A real fluid sink (continuity alone, no extra force) has v ~ r^-2 (scenario 3 above): it bends sound ~ b^-4, not b^-1.
